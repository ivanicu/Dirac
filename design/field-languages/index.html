Thirty-five ways to draw the same field, and what each one costs

Every tile below renders identical data: aspirin's classical electrostatic potential, one 96×96 slice through the molecular plane, computed by backend/field_server.py. Nothing here is a texture or a mock — if a language cannot survive real data it is not a language, it is a filter.

These are not thirty-five palettes. Thirty-five recolourings of one isosurface is the first thing anyone reaches for, which is exactly why it is worth nothing. What varies here is the mark: what the field is made of on the page. A contour is a line asserting equality. A stipple is a countable event. A hillshade is a lie about light that the eye reads as height. Each tradition below already solved “how do I show a continuous quantity” for a different medium and a different reader — and each one brings its own distortion, named underneath it.

One rule is enforced across all 35 and is worth stating because it is the easiest thing to lose: zero stays at zero. A diverging field normalised to its own min and max drifts the neutral region off-centre and the picture then asserts a charge the molecule does not have. Every tile normalises against max(|min|, |max|) instead.

Rendered live from design/field-languages/data.js. Regenerate the specimen with a different molecule or field by re-running the extraction against any cached cube — the renderers take the grid, not a picture.

On the transfer function. A 1/r Coulomb field collapses to near zero a couple of ångström out, so a linear ramp spends most of its range on the nuclei and leaves the frame blank — all 35 languages would be drawing the same empty square, and the comparison would be about nothing. Display therefore applies a signed power law, |v|0.42, preserving sign and zero. The data stays raw. That split is the point: raw data with a declared transfer is honest; pre-scaled data under a linear-looking legend is not, and it is the same error as a colour ramp that quietly renormalises per molecule.
